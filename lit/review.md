# Literature review

## 1. The problem in one sentence

Two instruments measure the same sample and return different numbers. The
question is not whether the numbers differ — they always do — but whether there
is a *principled* object, computable from the measurements, that does not.

This review covers five literatures that touch that question: measurement
theory, invariant theory, calibration transfer, domain adaptation, and
equivariant learning. It then surveys the physics of the instruments used in
this paper. The final section states plainly what is and is not new here.

## 2. Measurement theory: scales are groups

The modern theory of measurement is representational. A measurement is a
homomorphism from an empirical relational structure into a numerical one, and
the choice of homomorphism is unique only up to a group of admissible
transformations. Stevens (1946) made this operational with the familiar scale
types: nominal (permutation group), ordinal (monotone transformations), interval
(affine transformations \(x \mapsto ax+b\)), and ratio (multiplicative
transformations \(x \mapsto ax\)). Krantz, Luce, Suppes, and Tversky (1971)
gave the representation and uniqueness theorems that justify those labels.
Narens (1985) and Luce and Suppes (2002) extended the programme to extensive,
conjoint, and non-Archimedean structures.

The key lesson for instrument interoperability is simple and often forgotten:
**a scale type is a statement about which transformations leave the measurement
meaningful.** An interval scale admits a common affine change; a ratio scale
admits a common multiplicative change. If two instruments induce transformations
outside the admissible group, the measurements are not on the same scale, and no
amount of normalization can make them comparable without additional assumptions.

Klein's Erlanger Programm (1872) is the geometric twin of this idea. A geometry
is the pair (space, group), and its theorems are the invariants of the group
action. The present paper takes Klein literally: an instrument is a group acting
on a measurement space, and the device-invariant content of a measurement is the
quotient by that group.

## 3. Invariant theory: what survives a group action

Classical invariant theory studies the ring of polynomials fixed by a group
action. Weyl (1939) is the standard reference for the classical groups; Mumford,
Fogarty, and Kirwan (1994) give the geometric invariant theory (GIT) of quotients;
Olver (1995) develops the equivalence and symmetry methods used in differential
equations. Hilbert's finiteness theorem (1893) guarantees finite generation for
reductive groups over suitable fields.

Two technical points matter here.

First, **non-reductive groups can have trivial polynomial invariants but
non-trivial rational invariants.** The affine group \(\mathrm{Aff}(1)\) is the
semidirect product \(\mathbb{R}_{>0} \ltimes \mathbb{R}\); its translation
subgroup is unipotent, so GIT does not apply directly. A polynomial invariant
must be translation-invariant (hence a polynomial in differences) and
scale-invariant (hence homogeneous of degree zero), which forces it to be
constant. The rational invariant field, however, is generated by ratios of
differences. This distinction is not pedantry: it explains why the useful
device-invariant objects are ratios, not polynomial features.

Second, **the quotient dimension is a counting rule, not an information
measure.** For a free action of a Lie group \(G\) on an open dense set of a
manifold \(M\), \(\dim(M/G)=\dim M-\dim G\). If the group is as large as the
measurement space, the quotient is a point and no nonconstant invariant exists.
The rule is used throughout this paper to decide whether a proposed invariance
can carry any signal.

Shape analysis (Dryden & Mardia, 2016) provides the closest worked examples:
remove translation, scale, and rotation from a configuration of landmarks, and
the remaining shape is the quotient. Affine shape — the invariants of
\(\mathrm{Aff}(1)\) acting on points on a line — is exactly the object used in
the MOX analysis below.

## 4. Calibration transfer in chemometrics

Near-infrared (NIR) spectroscopy has confronted cross-instrument transfer for
decades, and its literature is the most mature source of practical methods.

The classical approaches are:

- **Direct standardization (DS)** and **piecewise direct standardization
  (PDS)**. A small set of reference samples measured on both instruments is used
  to estimate a transformation from the target spectra to the source spectra
  (Wang & Kowalski, 1992; Bouveresse & Massart, 1996). PDS fits a local
  regression around each wavelength, which handles wavelength-dependent
  response differences better than a global affine map.
- **Global affine / scatter correction.** Standard normal variate (SNV; Barnes,
  Dhanoa & Lister, 1989) and multiplicative scatter correction (MSC; Geladi,
  MacDougall & Martens, 1985) remove a common multiplicative and additive
  scatter. They are the practical form of a global affine invariance.
- **Reference-sample selection.** Kennard–Stone and related algorithms choose
  the calibration subset that spans the design space (Kennard & Stone, 1969;
  Shenk & Westerhaus, 1991). The choice of references is known to matter, which
  is why the NIR experiment below reports anchor conditioning explicitly.
- **Model updating and hybrid calibration.** Instead of transforming spectra,
  one can add target samples to the calibration set, or build a model with
  instrument-specific terms (Martens & Næs, 1989; Workman, 2018).

Fearn's review (2001) and Workman's update (2018) are the best entry points.
The consensus is that transfer works when the instruments are similar and the
reference set is representative; it degrades when the physical response changes
nonlinearly or when the reference set is too small.

The present paper does not propose a better PDS. It asks a prior question:
*which transformation group is the instrument entitled to induce?* PDS is a
section of a quotient; the quotient itself is the invariant. Once the group is
derived from physics, the minimum number of references and the conditions under
which no invariant exists follow from the group, not from trial and error.

## 5. Domain adaptation and transfer learning

Machine learning has its own literature on distribution shift. Ben-David et al.
(2010) bound target error by source error plus a divergence between domains plus
the error of the best joint hypothesis. Pan and Yang (2010) survey the field.
Representation-based methods include domain-adversarial training (Ganin et al.,
2016), correlation alignment (CORAL; Sun, Feng & Saenko, 2016), maximum mean
discrepancy (Gretton et al., 2012), and deep adaptation networks (Long et al.,
2015). Domain generalization (Zhou et al., 2023) targets unseen domains without
target data.

These methods are powerful but largely *phenomenological*: they learn a
representation that is empirically invariant across the domains in the training
set. They do not derive the invariance from the measurement physics, and they do
not tell you when the invariance is impossible. A domain-adversarial network can
remove a batch signature that is correlated with the label, and it can fail
silently when the target shift is outside the training distribution.

The framework here is complementary. It supplies the group that a domain-
adaptation method should quotient by, and it supplies a hardness result: if the
group is the full per-sensor affine group, the label is not invariant and no
representation can recover it from a single measurement.

## 6. Equivariant and geometric deep learning

Cohen and Welling (2016) introduced group-equivariant convolutional networks;
Kondor and Trivedi (2018) characterized equivariant linear maps for compact
groups; Bronstein et al. (2021) give a unified geometric view. Gauge-equivariant
networks (Cohen et al., 2019) make the invariance local, which is conceptually
close to the per-wavelength and per-channel groups used here.

The difference is one of scope. Equivariant networks *build in* a group that is
known or chosen; this paper *derives* the group from the instrument's physics and
then measures what the quotient retains. The two approaches compose: a derived
group can be used to design an equivariant architecture, and an equivariant
architecture can approximate an invariant that is expensive to compute
analytically.

## 7. Physics of the instruments

### 7.1 Metal-oxide semiconductor (MOX) gas sensors

The conduction model of Barsan and Weimar (2001, 2003) gives the response of a
MOX sensor to a reducing gas as a power law,
\[
  s = a\,C^{b},
\]
where \(C\) is concentration and \(a,b\) depend on the sensor material,
temperature, humidity, and aging. Rock, Barsan, and Weimar (2008) review the
device physics. The power law is an idealization; real sensors drift, saturate,
and respond to multiple gases. The UCI drift dataset (Vergara et al., 2012)
documents 36 months of such drift on a 16-sensor array. Fonollosa et al. (2015)
and Huerta et al. (2016) study compensation methods. Marco and
Gutiérrez-Galvez (2012) review signal processing for machine olfaction.

The important structural point is that \(a\) and \(b\) are *device* parameters.
A change of device maps \(\log s\) by an affine transformation. The full group
acts independently on each sensor, which is why gas identity is not invariant
under arbitrary sensor replacement. This is the mathematical form of the
"zero-shot cross-device transfer is impossible" result in the OpenSmell paper
(James, 2026).

### 7.2 NIR and optical spectroscopy

The Beer–Lambert law gives absorbance
\[
  A(\lambda) = \varepsilon(\lambda)\,c\,L + b(\lambda),
\]
where \(\varepsilon\) is the molar absorptivity, \(c\) the concentration, \(L\)
the path length, and \(b\) a baseline. Instrument differences enter as a
wavelength-dependent gain and offset, and sometimes as a wavelength shift:
\[
  A'(\lambda) = g(\lambda)A(\lambda) + o(\lambda), \qquad
  \lambda' = \lambda + \delta.
\]
The first is an affine group at each wavelength; the second is a translation of
the abscissa. Scattering adds a multiplicative and additive effect that is
smooth in wavelength. Burns and Ciurczak (2007), Siesler et al. (2002), and
Workman and Weyer (2012) are standard references. The Corn dataset used here is
distributed by Eigenvector Research and was originally measured at Cargill
(80 samples, 3 instruments, 700 wavelengths).

### 7.3 Mass spectrometry

A mass spectrum is a set of peaks \((m/z_i, I_i)\). Calibration differences are
approximately affine on the m/z axis, and detector differences are approximately
a gain on intensities. Fragmentation patterns depend on the instrument's
collision energy and ionization mode, which is *not* an affine transformation.
MassBank (Horai et al., 2010) and the Human Metabolome Database (Wishart et al.,
2022) are the main public repositories. The Minimum Reporting Standards
(Sumner et al., 2007) document the metadata needed for meaningful comparison.
Glish and Vachet (2003) give an accessible introduction to the physics.

### 7.4 EEG and MEG

EEG measures scalp potentials; MEG measures magnetic fields. Both are linear
mixtures of underlying sources, but the sensor gains, offsets, and reference
montages differ across headboxes. A simplified instrument model is a per-channel
affine map,
\[
  x'_c(t) = g_c x_c(t) + o_c,
\]
plus a common-reference transform \(x_c \mapsto x_c - x_{\mathrm{ref}}\). Nunez
and Srinivasan (2006) cover the neurophysics; Hämäläinen et al. (1993) cover MEG;
Gramfort et al. (2013) describe the MNE software used here; Schalk et al. (2004)
and Goldberger et al. (2000) document the PhysioNet data.

### 7.5 Microscopy

A fluorescence microscope image is affected by an illumination field, detector
gain and offset, and blur:
\[
  I'(x,y) = g(x,y)\,I(x,y) + o(x,y)
\]
with \(g,o\) smooth. Without smoothness this is a per-pixel affine group and no
within-image invariant exists; with smoothness, flat-field correction is the
quotient section. Caicedo et al. (2017) and Ljosa, Sokolnicki, and Carpenter
(2012) review image-based profiling and the BBBC datasets; van der Walt et al.
(2014) describe scikit-image.

### 7.6 DNA sequencing

Sequencing instruments apply a stochastic error channel to a discrete sequence:
substitutions, insertions, deletions, and context-dependent errors. The channel
is a linear map on k-mer frequency vectors only in the substitution-only,
position-independent case; indels and context effects make it nonlinear.
Goodwin, McPherson, and McCombie (2016) and Quince et al. (2017) review the
technology and its biases. The Genome in a Bottle consortium (Zook et al., 2020)
provides same-sample multi-platform benchmarks; those data are large, and the
present paper uses a simulation on the real phiX174 genome to demonstrate the
boundary.

## 8. The OpenSmell result

The companion paper (James, 2026, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21243013) introduces a
modular feature framework for MOX e-noses. Its Theorem 1 proves that the
resistance ratio \(R_s/R_0\) cancels the supply voltage and load resistance.
Its Theorem 2 proves that two devices with different power-law exponents produce
different normalized response curves, so zero-shot transfer of raw features is
impossible without calibration. It reports session-invariance on a single rig
(88.5%), a failure of zero-shot cross-device transfer, and the falsification of a
four-category response-type scheme.

The present paper is consistent with that result and explains it structurally.
Theorem 2 is a statement about the *raw response*: the map between devices is
affine in log space, and the raw response is not invariant under it. The
invariant objects are ratios of log differences; they are device-invariant but
lower-dimensional. For MOX, the full per-sensor affine group makes gas identity
unobservable; the useful invariants exist only under a restricted group (for
example, per-sensor gain with fixed exponent). This is why the honest value of
invariance for MOX is interoperability and drift robustness, not molecular
identification.

## 9. What is actually new

This paper is a synthesis, not a discovery of invariance. The ingredients —
Klein's program, Stevens' scales, invariant theory, calibration transfer, domain
adaptation, equivariant networks — are old and well developed. The contribution
is the combination and the discipline it imposes:

1. **A formal instrument-group framework.** Measurement space, group action,
   quotient, invariant field, and observability are defined once and used across
   modalities.
2. **Physics-derived groups.** The groups are derived from the instrument
   equations (power law, Beer–Lambert, per-channel amplifier, m/z calibration,
   illumination field, sequencing error channel), not chosen for convenience.
3. **An observability boundary.** The paper proves that the full per-sensor MOX
   group has trivial polynomial invariants and makes gas identity unobservable;
   that per-wavelength affine spectroscopy needs two references and has a
   non-trivial quotient; and that the sequencing error channel is not affine, so
   no low-dimensional invariant exists in general.
4. **A dataset-fitness table.** The paper states which public datasets actually
   support cross-instrument claims and which do not. This is a contribution in a
   field where "multi-device" is often claimed for temporal drift on one device.
5. **Honest negative results.** Real Raman data with 17 spectra are underpowered;
   MOX cross-batch transfer improves only modestly; the full MOX group destroys
   gas identity. These are reported as results, not hidden.

## 10. What this paper does not claim

- It does not claim that invariance solves interoperability. It gives the
  conditions under which an invariant exists and the price of quotienting.
- It does not claim that the derived groups are exact. They are models; the
  experiments measure their adequacy and their failures.
- It does not claim that the synthetic instrument transforms are recordings from
  real second instruments. Every such experiment is labelled.
- It does not claim priority. Calibration transfer, invariant representations,
  and domain adaptation are mature fields; the synthesis is the contribution.
