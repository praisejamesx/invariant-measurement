# Two rulers, one table: a plain-English guide to instrument invariants

This is the companion explainer for the paper *Instrument Groups and
Invariants*. It assumes no mathematics beyond ratios and averages. Every term
is defined where it first appears. If you want the formal version, read
`paper/draft.md`; if you want the intuition first, read this.

---

## 1. The problem: the table did not change, the ruler did

Imagine three people measure the same table with three different rulers.

- Ruler A says the table is 2.0 units long.
- Ruler B says it is 4.0 units long.
- Ruler C says it is 3.0 units long.

Did the table change? No. The rulers are different. Maybe ruler A is marked in
metres, ruler B in half-metres, ruler C in something else. Maybe each ruler
starts its numbers at a different place. The readings differ, but the table is
the same.

This is the everyday version of the problem this paper studies. An instrument
is a ruler. Two instruments measuring the same sample give different numbers.
The question is: **is there a number you can compute from the readings that is
the same for all rulers?**

For three rulers, yes:

\[
  \frac{2-4}{3-4} = 2.
\]

If every ruler changes its numbers by the same rule — multiply by some number
and add some number — that ratio stays 2. Try it: replace \(2,4,3\) by
\(2k+d, 4k+d, 3k+d\). The ratio is still 2. The ratio is an **invariant**.

An invariant is a quantity that does not change when the instrument changes in
an allowed way. The key phrase is "allowed way." If the ruler can change in any
way at all, no invariant exists. The science is in deciding what "allowed"
means.

---

## A short vocabulary (skip if you know the words)

- **MOX:** metal-oxide semiconductor gas sensor. A gas changes the sensor's
  resistance; the response is roughly a power law in concentration.
- **NIR:** near-infrared spectroscopy. Light absorption by molecular overtones;
  used for rapid, non-destructive measurement of materials.
- **Raman:** Raman spectroscopy. Light scatters inelastically from molecular
  vibrations; the spectrum is a fingerprint of the sample.
- **Mass spectrometry:** a sample is ionized, the ions are separated by
  mass-to-charge ratio, and the peak pattern is measured.
- **EEG:** electroencephalography. Voltages at scalp electrodes, generated by
  brain activity.
- **MEG:** magnetoencephalography. Magnetic fields outside the head, generated
  by the same brain activity.
- **DNA sequencing:** reading the order of the four bases (A, C, G, T) in a DNA
  molecule.
- **R²:** the fraction of variance explained by a prediction. 1 is perfect; 0 is
  no better than predicting the mean; negative is worse than the mean.
- **Dice overlap:** a segmentation score. 1 is perfect overlap with the
  ground-truth mask; 0 is no overlap.
- **k-mer:** a short DNA subsequence of length \(k\). A 5-mer is a sequence of
  five bases.
- **Haplotype:** one version of a genomic region. Two haplotypes differ by a
  small number of bases.
- **PDS:** piecewise direct standardization, a calibration method that fits a
  local map around each wavelength instead of one global map.
- **Affine:** multiply by a scale and add an offset: \(y\mapsto ky+d\).
- **Group, orbit, quotient, section:** defined in Sections 2–5; the short
  version is that a group is the set of allowed instrument changes, an orbit is
  all readings an instrument can produce for one sample, the quotient is the
  set of orbits, and a section picks one representative per orbit.

## 2. What an instrument is allowed to do

Different instruments change measurements in different ways. The paper calls
the set of allowed changes a **group**. A group is just a collection of
transformations that you can combine and undo. The transformations in this
paper are mostly simple:

- **Multiply by a number.** A ruler with a different scale.
- **Add a number.** A ruler with a different zero.
- **Multiply and add.** Both at once. This is called an **affine**
  transformation: \(y \mapsto ky+d\), where \(k\) is the scale and \(d\) is the
  offset.

For the table example, each ruler has its own \(k\) and \(d\). The ratio of
differences is invariant under a *common* affine change: the same \(k\) and
\(d\) for all three readings. If each ruler changes independently, the ratio is
not invariant; you need a different invariant, or more information.

That distinction — common change versus independent change — is the heart of
the paper. Most confusion about "normalization" comes from not saying which one
is meant.

---

## 3. Logs: turning multiplication into addition

A metal-oxide gas sensor responds to a gas concentration \(C\) roughly like

\[
  s = a\,C^{b}.
\]

Here \(s\) is the sensor reading, \(C\) is the concentration, and \(a\) and
\(b\) are numbers that depend on the sensor. The exponent \(b\) is the important
one: it says how strongly the sensor responds. Different sensors have different
\(a\) and different \(b\).

A power law is hard to compare across sensors because the exponent changes the
shape of the curve. But if you take the logarithm of both sides,

\[
  \log s = \log a + b\log C.
\]

Now the relationship is a straight line: \(\log s\) is \(b\) times
\(\log C\) plus a constant. Multiplication has become addition. This is why the
paper works in **log space** for MOX sensors. In log space, changing the sensor
is an affine change:

\[
  y_B = k\,y_A + d,
\]

where \(y=\log s\). The scale \(k\) is the ratio of the exponents, and the
offset \(d\) combines the two \(a\)'s. The algebra is in Theorem 1 of the paper.

Once the instrument change is affine, the table logic applies. Three
measurements at three concentrations give an invariant:

\[
  \frac{y_1-y_2}{y_3-y_2}
  =
  \frac{\log C_1-\log C_2}{\log C_3-\log C_2}.
\]

The sensor's \(a\) and \(b\) cancel. The invariant is the shape of the response
curve, expressed as a ratio of log-concentration differences. It is the same
for every sensor that obeys the same power law.

But — and this is the crucial "but" — the invariant is not the gas identity. It
is the shape of the curve. Two different gases can have the same curve shape on
one sensor. To tell gases apart, you need to compare several sensors. That is a
different problem, and it has a different group.

## 4. Orbits: all the readings an instrument could give

Here is a useful picture. Imagine a space of all possible measurements. A
single sample is a point in that space. When you change the instrument, the
point moves. The set of all points you can reach by changing the instrument in
allowed ways is called the **orbit** of the sample.

- If the allowed change is "multiply by a positive number," the orbit is a
  ray from the origin.
- If the allowed change is "multiply and add," the orbit is a line.
- If every sensor can change independently, the orbit is much bigger.

The **quotient** is the set of orbits. Two measurements are in the same orbit
if one can be turned into the other by an allowed instrument change. The
quotient is the device-invariant description: it forgets which point on the
orbit you saw and keeps only which orbit it was.

An invariant is a function that is constant on each orbit. It is a label for
the orbit. The quotient is the complete set of such labels.

Now the key question: **how big is the orbit?**

For a single MOX measurement with \(p\) sensors, if each sensor can change its
offset independently, the orbit is the whole space. Any reading can be turned
into any other reading by choosing the offsets. The quotient is a single point.
There is no invariant. The gas identity is not recoverable, because the
instrument can imitate any gas by changing its offsets.

This is Theorem 3 of the paper. It sounds negative, and it is. It says that
"arbitrary per-sensor affine replacement" is too large a group for gas
identification. The useful invariants require a smaller group: for example,
assume the exponent \(b\) is stable and only a per-sensor multiplicative gain
in the linear response changes (an additive shift in log space). Then the
slopes are unchanged and the slope ratios \(b_i/b_j\) survive. That is a physical assumption, not a mathematical
universality.

The lesson: **the group is a scientific claim.** If you choose a group that is
too large, you lose the label. If you choose one that is too small, it will not
cover the real instrument variation. The paper's job is to derive the group
from physics and then measure what survives.

## 5. Calibration is a choice of representative

The quotient tells you which orbit you are on. Often you want a number in the
original units, not just an orbit label. To get that, you need a **section**: a
rule that picks one representative from each orbit.

The table example: the ratio 2 tells you the shape of the three readings, but
not the table length in metres. To get metres, measure a known object on each
ruler. That known object is a **reference**. It chooses a representative.

Calibration transfer is exactly this. You measure a few reference samples on
both instruments, estimate the transformation, and map new samples into the
source instrument's frame. The transformation is the section. The invariant is
what you would have if you did not need the reference.

This distinction explains a lot of confusing results:

- A method can be a good **section** and a bad **invariant**. It may reconstruct
  the source numbers accurately but still depend on the reference set.
- A method can be a good **invariant** and a bad **section**. It may be stable
  across instruments but lose the absolute scale that a regression needs.
- More references help the section, not the invariant. In the NIR experiment,
  the invariant is flat at about 0.83 whether you use two references or twenty;
  calibration improves from 0.857 to 0.926 as you add references. The invariant
  only needs two points to define the quotient; the section can use more.

---

## 6. How many references do you need?

The number is a property of the group, not of the algorithm.

- **Global affine** (one scale and one offset for the whole spectrum): two
  numbers to estimate, so two references suffice.
- **Per-wavelength affine** (a different scale and offset at every wavelength):
  two numbers *per wavelength*, so at first it looks like \(2p\) references.
  But the same two reference samples are measured at every wavelength, so two
  *samples* suffice for the whole spectrum. That is Theorem 4.
- **Per-pixel affine** (a different scale and offset at every pixel): \(2d\)
  numbers for \(d\) pixels. Without a smoothness assumption, you would need
  \(d\) references, which is impractical. Microscopy therefore assumes the
  illumination field is smooth, and estimates it from a blank or from the
  image's own background.

The NIR experiment makes this concrete. The per-wavelength invariant uses two
reference samples and reaches R² 0.83–0.91. Calibration with five references
reaches 0.886–0.933. PDS, a local calibration method, reaches 0.697–0.905. The
invariant is the most stable across instrument pairs; calibration is slightly
more accurate on average. Neither is universally better.

## 7. Why the anchor matters

The invariant divides by the difference between the two reference samples at
each wavelength:

\[
  r_{i\lambda}
  =
  \frac{A_{i\lambda}-A_{1\lambda}}{A_{2\lambda}-A_{1\lambda}}.
\]

If the two references are nearly identical at some wavelength, the denominator
is small, and noise is amplified. The NIR experiment shows this dramatically:
random reference pairs have a median R² of −0.073, while a well-conditioned
pair reaches 0.824. The anchor choice is part of the algorithm, not a detail.

This is a general lesson. An invariant can be mathematically exact and
numerically terrible. The paper reports both the algebra and the conditioning.

## 8. What happens in each modality

### 8.1 NIR: the strongest evidence

The Corn dataset has 80 physical samples measured on three real NIR
instruments. This is the strongest evidence in the paper because the same
samples are measured on all three. The per-wavelength affine group explains
almost all of the cross-instrument variation: an oracle fit reaches R² 0.9985
or better. Raw transfer fails badly for the distant instrument pair (R² as low
as −9.85). The two-reference invariant is stable across all six ordered pairs
(0.834–0.910). The invariant does not need target labels; it needs two
reference spectra.

This is the case where the framework works as advertised. The group is
adequate, the number of references is predicted by the group, and the
invariant transfers.

### 8.2 MOX: invariance is real but the group is dangerous

The UCI MOX dataset is one 16-sensor array measured over 36 months. It is not
three different instruments; it is one instrument drifting. The paper labels it
a "temporal pseudo-device."

The full per-sensor affine group is transitive on a single measurement: any
reading can be mapped to any other. Gas identity is not observable. A smaller
group — per-sensor multiplicative gain in the linear response, with the
exponent fixed — leaves slope ratios, and those are stable for five of six
gases (median relative MAD below 0.10); gas 3
is the exception (0.285).

Empirically, per-batch accuracy is 0.390 raw, 0.461 log, 0.482 normalized
selectivity, 0.451 exact per-sensor gain (batch centering), and 0.711 with five
labelled target samples per class. In the pooled table, batch centering is
0.515, the best unsupervised method. The exact invariant is competitive, not
magic. The labelled calibration wins because it uses labels.

The MOX lesson is that "device-invariant" is not the same as "identifies the
gas." The full group destroys the label; the restricted group preserves a
lower-dimensional invariant; the invariant is useful for drift robustness but
not sufficient for molecular identification.

### 8.3 EEG: the baseline trick

EEG measures voltages at many scalp electrodes. A different amplifier has a
different gain and offset at each channel. If you compute band power and take
logs, a per-channel gain adds a constant. Subtract the log power from a
baseline period, and the constant cancels:

\[
  \log P_c(\text{task})-\log P_c(\text{baseline})
  =
  \log\frac{P_c(\text{task})}{P_c(\text{baseline})}.
\]

This is the EEG version of the MOX \(R_s/R_0\) ratio. It is a reference
measurement, not a label. In the experiment, the baseline representation is the
best under gain+offset (0.834 vs 0.686 raw). But a common-reference transform
mixes channels, so the per-channel cancellation no longer works; baseline drops
to 0.756 and the baseline ratio to 0.544. The group boundary is visible.

### 8.4 Raman: too few samples

The real Raman data have 17 spectra (7 healthy, 10 diseased). The invariant is
better than raw under a synthetic affine transform, but worse than raw under
the identity transform. With 17 samples, the confidence intervals are wide. The
paper reports this as a negative/underpowered result, not a success. A
controlled synthetic experiment with 600 spectra shows the mechanism
(invariant 0.640 vs raw 0.522), but that is evidence about the algorithm, not
about a real Raman instrument.

### 8.5 Microscopy: smoothness is the section

A microscope image is affected by a smooth illumination gradient and an offset.
The per-pixel affine group is too large, but the smooth field can be estimated
from the image's own large-scale background. Flat-field correction raises Dice
overlap from 0.894 to 0.965 (oracle 0.987). The smoothness assumption is what
makes the section possible.

### 8.6 Mass spectrometry: two peaks

Mass spectra have peaks at m/z values. A different calibration shifts the m/z
axis; a different detector changes the intensity gain. Two reference peaks
determine the affine m/z map. In a synthetic controlled experiment, raw
transfer under an m/z shift is unstable (0.777 ± 0.279), while two-peak
calibration is 1.000. Fragmentation changes peak intensities in a way that is
not affine; calibration drops to 0.970. The experiment is synthetic because no
paired public sample set was available.

### 8.7 Sequencing: the negative case

DNA sequencing does not fit the affine story. The instrument applies a
stochastic error channel: substitutions, insertions, deletions, and
context-dependent errors. With substitutions only, the channel is linear on
k-mer frequencies and can be inverted if the error matrix is known. With
indels or context effects, it is nonlinear. In the phiX174 simulation, coarse
regional classification is robust (about 1.0), but fine haplotype
classification is not rescued by platform normalization (0.63–0.65 vs 0.64–0.67
raw). The channel is not a multiplicative bias; the affine-style correction is
the wrong model.

This is the most important negative result. It shows that the framework is not
a universal recipe. When the instrument is not affine, the quotient
construction does not apply, and the honest response is a probabilistic error
model, not a normalization.

## 9. Why this matters for digital olfaction

Electronic noses are often described as "cross-device" when the data are
actually one device drifting over time. The paper's fitness table separates the
two. It also explains why "just normalize the response" is not enough: the
normalization is a group choice, and the choice determines whether the gas
label survives.

The companion OpenSmell paper proves that raw normalized MOX responses cannot
transfer zero-shot across devices with different power-law exponents. This
paper does not contradict that result; it explains it. The raw response is not
invariant. The invariant is a lower-dimensional object: ratios of log
differences, slope ratios, or a batch-centered representation. Those objects
are useful for interoperability and drift robustness, but they are not a free
lunch for molecular identification.

The practical recommendation is:

1. Write down the instrument equation.
2. Derive the group.
3. Count dimensions. If the group is as large as the measurement, no
   continuous invariant exists.
4. Choose the invariant and check that the label is not invariant under the
   group.
5. If you need absolute scale, calibrate with references.
6. Condition the references.
7. Report the ceiling and the information used.

## 10. A five-minute summary

- Two instruments give different numbers for the same sample. That is normal.
- An **invariant** is a number that stays the same when the instrument changes
  in an allowed way.
- The allowed changes form a **group**. The group is derived from the physics
  of the instrument, not chosen arbitrarily.
- The set of all readings an instrument can give for one sample is an **orbit**.
  The **quotient** is the set of orbits; it is the device-invariant
  description.
- A **section** picks one representative per orbit. Calibration is a section.
- Invariants and calibration are dual: the invariant forgets the instrument,
  the section chooses one.
- The number of references is a group property: two for a global affine group,
  two samples for a per-wavelength affine group, many for a per-pixel group.
- The group can be too large. For a single MOX measurement, the full
  per-sensor affine group is transitive: any reading can be mapped to any
  other, so gas identity is not observable.
- The group can be too small. For EEG, a per-channel affine group does not
  include a common-reference transform, so the invariant breaks under a
  reference change.
- The group can be wrong. For sequencing, the error channel is not affine, and
  an affine-style normalization does not recover fine-grained signal.
- NIR is the strongest real cross-instrument evidence: the per-wavelength
  affine group is adequate, two references suffice, and the invariant transfers
  at R² 0.83–0.91.
- MOX is a temporal pseudo-device, not three instruments. The exact per-sensor
  gain invariant (batch centering) is competitive but not a substitute for
  calibration.
- Raman is underpowered; microscopy is a clean smooth-field demonstration; mass
  spec and sequencing are controlled synthetic/simulated demonstrations.
- The framework does not replace calibration transfer or domain adaptation. It
  tells you which group those methods are assuming, and when no invariant can
  exist.

## 11. Glossary

**Affine transformation.** A transformation of the form \(y \mapsto ky+d\):
multiply by a scale \(k\), then add an offset \(d\). It is the mathematics of a
ruler with a different zero and a different unit.

**Calibration transfer.** Using reference samples measured on two instruments
to map one instrument's measurements into the other's frame. In this paper, a
calibration is a section of the quotient.

**Group.** A collection of transformations that can be combined and undone. The
group is the set of changes an instrument is allowed to make.

**Invariant.** A quantity that does not change under the group. It labels the
orbit.

**Log space.** The space of logarithms of the measurements. Multiplication
becomes addition, and a power law becomes a straight line. Used for MOX
responses.

**Orbit.** The set of all measurements an instrument can produce for the same
sample by changing its allowed parameters.

**Per-sensor / per-channel / per-wavelength.** The group parameter is allowed to
differ for each sensor, channel, or wavelength. This is a larger group than a
single global parameter.

**Quotient.** The set of orbits. It is the complete device-invariant
description: two measurements are the same point in the quotient if one can be
turned into the other by an allowed instrument change.

**Reference sample.** A sample measured on both instruments, used to estimate
the transformation (the section). It supplies the absolute scale that the
invariant forgets.

**Section.** A rule that chooses one representative from each orbit. A
calibration is a section: it maps an invariant back into a chosen instrument's
frame.

**Synthetic instrument.** A transformation applied to real measurements to
simulate a second instrument. It is labelled as synthetic; it tests the
algorithm, not the instrument.

**Temporal pseudo-device.** A single instrument measured at different times.
The "device change" is drift, not different hardware. The UCI MOX data are a
temporal pseudo-device.
